Start with one number

1000 A

to a single processor die

A 1 kW accelerator running at a 1 V core rail draws

I = P / V = 1000 W / 1 V

Current, not power, is what the package has to survive. Every conductor between the board and the die carries some share of that 1000 A.

The engineering problem is not generating the power. It is delivering it.
Anchor the audience in a number they can feel. 1000 A is comparable to an arc welder. Do not move on until they accept that the current, not the wattage, is the problem.

Why low-voltage lateral delivery is so expensive

Ploss = Irms2 R

Loss grows with the square of current. Halving the delivery voltage doubles the current and quadruples the loss in the same copper.

Deliver at 1 V
1000 A across the whole board. Even 1 mΩ of path resistance burns 1 kW — as much as the chip uses.
Deliver at 48 V
~21 A for the same power. The same 1 mΩ burns under half a watt.

So: carry 48 V as far as possible, and convert to 1 V as close to the die as physically possible.

This slide is the whole motivation. The 48x voltage ratio is a 2300x loss ratio in the same conductor. Everything afterwards is a consequence of wanting the conversion point closer to the die.

Three ways to deliver the power

Lateral

Regulators at the board edge. The 1 V rail travels centimetres across the board and up through the package.

LONG HIGH-CURRENT PATH

Vertical (VPD)

Regulator moved underneath the die. The 1 V rail travels vertically through the package instead of laterally.

SHORTER PATH

Distributed vertical (DVPD)

Many small regulators spread under the die area, each feeding its own region. The 1 V rail is split 49 ways before it travels anywhere.

SHORT AND SPLIT

This work studies DVPD: 49 regulator cells on a 7×7 grid, each delivering about 20 A.
Make clear DVPD is not just 'closer' but 'divided'. Splitting the current 49 ways cuts I^2R by 49 in the distributed segment even before shortening it.

Packaging 101 — the physical stack

Arrow width suggests current magnitude: thin at 48 V, thick at 1 V.

Walk bottom to top once, slowly. PCB, BGA balls, substrate, RDL, micro-pads, die, TSVs, TIM, heat sink. Then trace the current: 48 V enters through a few balls, a VR cell converts it, and 1 V goes up into the die over a very short, very wide path. The audience must hold this picture for the rest of the talk.

The conductors this work has to account for

  • BGA ball — solder sphere joining package to board. 400 µm diameter here.
  • Package RDL — thin copper planes inside the package that spread current sideways.
  • Source pad — the small copper landing where a regulator meets the die interface.
  • TSV — a copper via straight through the silicon.
Every one of them obeys

R = ρ L / A

resistivity × length ÷ cross-section. The entire electrical model is this expression applied honestly to real geometry — plus a network solve wherever current can choose between parallel paths.

KEY IDEA if a conductor exists physically, it must have exactly one owner in the loss ledger — no double counting, and no conductor silently set to zero.

This slide sets up the discipline used later: every physical conductor has one owner. A reviewer asking 'what carries the return current?' should be answered by the ledger.

Two architectures under comparison

The cutout removes 841 of 1369 legal BGA sites — the central ones.

The single most important visual difference: Z2's cutout deletes exactly the BGA sites nearest the die centre. That turns out to matter for convergence later, because Z1's placement rule puts balls underneath regulator cells and Z2's geometrically cannot.

The converter inside each cell

48 V → 3:1 switched capacitor → 16 V → two-phase buck → 1 V

Why a hybrid? A single buck stage at 48→1 V needs a ~2 % duty cycle, which is very hard to do efficiently. The capacitor stage does the bulk ratio with no magnetics; the buck stage then regulates over a sane ratio.

  • 49 cells, ~20.4 A each
  • switching at 805 kHz
  • 250 nH inductors
  • tile 2.3685 mm square
The switching-state sequence is not in the converter model — this becomes the single largest open item in the talk.
Do not teach SC operation in detail. The one thing they must retain is that the model gives average currents and an output ripple, but never an input current waveform. Flag it here so the later OPEN item is not a surprise.

Why a power-delivery talk
needs mechanics

Current makes heat. Heat makes materials expand. Materials expand by different amounts. The package bends, and the solder joints holding it together deform — permanently.

Transition slide. The committee may be electrical engineers. Tell them the next six slides are deliberately first-principles and they should stop you if anything is unclear.

Thermal expansion mismatch (CTE)

εthermal = α ΔT

Every material has a coefficient of thermal expansion α. Silicon and organic package material differ by roughly 6×. Bonded together and heated, they cannot both have the length they want — so the assembly carries internal stress and bends.

This is the root cause of every mechanical result later. The 6x mismatch between silicon and organic substrate is the number to remember.

Mismatch alone does not predict bending

Young's modulus E measures how hard a material resists being deformed. A compliant layer can absorb mismatch; a stiff one forces the neighbour to move instead.

So warpage depends on all of:

  • CTE of each layer
  • stiffness E of each layer
  • thickness of each layer
  • overall geometry
  • how the package is held — the boundary conditions
Why this matters here
Z1 and Z2 move the regulator between the substrate and the board side. That changes the thickness and stiffness distribution of the stack, not just where the heat is made. Two architectures at the same temperature can bend differently.
Pre-empt the naive expectation that the hotter architecture must be mechanically worse. That expectation is wrong and the historical result shows it.

Warpage

Warpage is global out-of-plane bowing of the package. It is measured peak-to-valley, in micrometres.

It matters because it controls whether the package sits flat enough to assemble, how much load each BGA ball takes, and how far the corner joints are stretched.

Keep this short and visual. Mention that warpage sign conventions differ between vendors, so always state what is being measured.

Stress, strain, and the three kinds of deformation

Strain — how much something deformed, relative to its original size. Dimensionless.

Stress — the internal force per unit area needed to hold that deformation. Units MPa.

  • ELASTIC springs back completely when unloaded.
  • PLASTIC permanent; does not come back.
  • CREEP keeps deforming under a steady load, as a function of time and temperature.
Solder at operating temperature is at a large fraction of its melting point. It creeps continuously. Treating it as elastic would miss the damage mechanism entirely — so it is modelled as viscoplastic.
The viscoplastic point justifies the separate nonlinear local solder model. If asked why a 488-element submodel is worth a separate solve, this is the answer.

Global driver → local response

GLOBAL DRIVER
Δuc, the relative in-plane displacement imposed across the critical joint, plus its temperature. Units: µm.
LOCAL RESPONSE
inelastic strain, strain range, inelastic work density returned by the viscoplastic joint model. Dimensionless / MJ·m-3.

These are different quantities and are never reported as one.

This is the most commonly confused point in the whole project. The global model cannot resolve a solder joint; the local model cannot know the package. Say explicitly that a larger displacement driver does not automatically mean a larger local response.

Two modelling choices that can flip the answer

Stress-free temperature

The temperature at which the assembly is assumed mechanically relaxed. All differential thermal strain is measured from it.

Choose it 50 K higher and every predicted deformation changes. It is an assumption, not a measurement, and must be stated.

Boundary conditions

A package floating freely and the same package bolted to a board bend differently — sometimes in opposite directions.

A warpage number without its constraint description is not interpretable.

Both are declared explicitly in this framework rather than inherited silently from a solver default.
Short slide, but it pre-empts the classic reviewer question 'what was your reference temperature'. Having an answer ready signals rigour.

Three defects found in the last two phases

1 · Equal current sharing was an input

The solver injected Itotal/N at every ball, so the "result" that sharing is equal was the right-hand side. Replaced with a voltage-fed network: a rail feeds each ball through its own resistance, and the split became an output.

2 · The return conductor was discarded

Return sites were selected and never solved. Adding the return raises the loop resistance substantially — every earlier package resistance was the supply leg only.

3 · A floating-point bug hid 79 % of the design space

Lattice coordinates were built as -14.4 + i×0.8, which drifts. The mirror of (-14.4, -12.8) existed as (14.4, 12.800000000000002). The placement rule tests membership with exact equality, so it silently discarded 1080 of 1369 legal sites. Two headline numbers had been computed inside the surviving 21 %.

Defect 3 is the best story in the talk. It produced no error, no warning, and no failing test — the design space was simply smaller than anyone thought, and the subset was selected by floating-point luck. Mention that the fix is a picometre snap plus a permanent closure assertion.

Questions

Appendix — the voltage-fed network

Unknowns: sheet node voltages, one node per ball, one per cell pad, one rail node held at a fixed potential. Conductances:

  • sheet edge: 1/Rsq, with Rsq = ρCu(T) / (tCu · occupancy)
  • rail → ball j: 1/Rball, Rball = ρsolder h / A = 0.3104 mΩ from Table III geometry
  • ball → sheet node k: Aoverlap,k / ρc

Loads are drawn at the cell pad nodes. Ball currents are therefore an output: Ij = (Vrail − VBj) / Rball.

Identities checked every solve: ΣIball = Itotal to ~1e-10 A, and ΣgΔV² = V·I to ~1e-10 relative, with Reff = P / I².

Appendix — convergence criterion

Declared before any value was inspected: 1 % between the two finest grids on all five of

r_eff_ohm · p_loss_w · max_cell_drop_v · i_ball_max_a · ball_imax_over_imean

Reff is an integral of the field and settles first; local quantities settle last. At HEAD, Z1 return drifts 0.849 % on Reff — which would pass — but 1.069 % on maximum cell drop, which fails. Checking Reff alone would have declared that network converged.

A 400 µm ball needs h ≤ 50 µm, so the resolved grid is n = 601 over the 30 mm substrate. The earlier study stopped at h = 0.3 mm, where the ball was sub-grid.

Appendix — why the 48 V RMS cannot be closed

The pinned generator builds every electrical quantity from one scalar, I_out_rms = sqrt(I_out² + Δi²/12), the triangular-ripple identity on the output inductor, then duty-weights it.

It contains no switched-capacitor state sequence, no flying-capacitor charge balance, and no input current variable at all. The 48 V average therefore comes only from power balance — and average current closes energy balance while RMS closes conduction loss. One does not give the other.

Closing it requires the per-state switch conduction table, the charge-transfer schedule, or a netlist of the adopted topology.

Hybrid/SC RMS papers derive input RMS correctly for their topologies — methodology context only. Importing their ratio would be transferring a different converter's waveform.
