## Slide 1 — Electrothermal–Mechanical Co-Design ofDistributed Vertical Power Delivery
Open by saying the talk has three parts: why vertical power delivery is forced on us, how the framework evaluates a package across three physics domains, and what physical closure changed about the conclusions. Say explicitly that several earlier numerical results were withdrawn during this work and that this is the point of the talk.

## Slide 2 — Start with one number
Anchor the audience in a number they can feel. 1000 A is comparable to an arc welder. Do not move on until they accept that the current, not the wattage, is the problem.

## Slide 3 — Why low-voltage lateral delivery is so expensive
This slide is the whole motivation. The 48x voltage ratio is a 2300x loss ratio in the same conductor. Everything afterwards is a consequence of wanting the conversion point closer to the die.

## Slide 4 — Three ways to deliver the power
Make clear DVPD is not just 'closer' but 'divided'. Splitting the current 49 ways cuts I^2R by 49 in the distributed segment even before shortening it.

## Slide 5 — Packaging 101 — the physical stack
Walk bottom to top once, slowly. PCB, BGA balls, substrate, RDL, micro-pads, die, TSVs, TIM, heat sink. Then trace the current: 48 V enters through a few balls, a VR cell converts it, and 1 V goes up into the die over a very short, very wide path. The audience must hold this picture for the rest of the talk.

## Slide 6 — The conductors this work has to account for
This slide sets up the discipline used later: every physical conductor has one owner. A reviewer asking 'what carries the return current?' should be answered by the ledger.

## Slide 7 — Two architectures under comparison
The single most important visual difference: Z2's cutout deletes exactly the BGA sites nearest the die centre. That turns out to matter for convergence later, because Z1's placement rule puts balls underneath regulator cells and Z2's geometrically cannot.

## Slide 8 — The converter inside each cell
Do not teach SC operation in detail. The one thing they must retain is that the model gives average currents and an output ripple, but never an input current waveform. Flag it here so the later OPEN item is not a surprise.

## Slide 9 — (section divider)
This is the thesis statement slide. Pause here. The feedback arrow between electrical and thermal is why a fixed-point iteration is needed rather than a single pass.

## Slide 10 — The model hierarchy — what is analytical, what is FEM
Expect the question 'why not just use Ansys for everything'. The answer is on this slide: the expensive stages are kept, the cheap ones are only used where a reduced model has been checked. Do not claim the framework replaces FEM.

## Slide 11 — How one architecture is evaluated
The loop-vs-once point is a real design decision and a likely question. Say that thermoelastic deformation does not change the loss, so it is a one-way coupling.

## Slide 12 — (section divider)
Transition slide. The committee may be electrical engineers. Tell them the next six slides are deliberately first-principles and they should stop you if anything is unclear.

## Slide 13 — Thermal expansion mismatch (CTE)
This is the root cause of every mechanical result later. The 6x mismatch between silicon and organic substrate is the number to remember.

## Slide 14 — Mismatch alone does not predict bending
Pre-empt the naive expectation that the hotter architecture must be mechanically worse. That expectation is wrong and the historical result shows it.

## Slide 15 — Warpage
Keep this short and visual. Mention that warpage sign conventions differ between vendors, so always state what is being measured.

## Slide 16 — Stress, strain, and the three kinds of deformation
The viscoplastic point justifies the separate nonlinear local solder model. If asked why a 488-element submodel is worth a separate solve, this is the answer.

## Slide 17 — Global driver &rarr; local response
This is the most commonly confused point in the whole project. The global model cannot resolve a solder joint; the local model cannot know the package. Say explicitly that a larger displacement driver does not automatically mean a larger local response.

## Slide 18 — Response is not damage, and damage is not life
This is the defensive slide for the mechanics. If someone asks 'how many cycles will it survive', the answer is that no absolute life is claimed and why — Darveaux is the citation. Relative ordering between two architectures is still meaningful.

## Slide 19 — Two modelling choices that can flip the answer
Short slide, but it pre-empts the classic reviewer question 'what was your reference temperature'. Having an answer ready signals rigour.

## Slide 20 — (section divider)
Set the tone: this is a methods contribution. Each defect below changed a number that had already been written up. Do not rush these slides — a committee respects a candidate who found their own errors.

## Slide 21 — What physical closure actually changed
Do not read every row. Pick three: the RMS bound inversion, the pad tautology, and the mismatched ball count. Say each one had already produced a number that was reported.

## Slide 22 — Three defects found in the last two phases
Defect 3 is the best story in the talk. It produced no error, no warning, and no failing test — the design space was simply smaller than anyone thought, and the subset was selected by floating-point luck. Mention that the fix is a picometre snap plus a permanent closure assertion.

## Slide 23 — BGA current sharing — assumption vs solve
The load here is uniform across all 49 cells, so every bit of the non-uniformity comes from the network geometry. This is the slide that invalidates the old sizing rule.

## Slide 24 — How many BGA balls does each architecture actually need?
Three things to point at: the curves are not 1/N because changing N changes which sites are selected; the 1.5 A line is ours, not a datasheet; and the higher-RMS scenarios do not come down to the line at any legal count for Z2.

## Slide 25 — BGA count — the closure
If asked why not simply pick the largest count: more balls is not free — it consumes lattice sites, routing area and board real estate, and for Z2 the cutout limits what is geometrically available.

## Slide 26 — The cutout penalty is smaller than where you put the balls
This number has now been corrected twice, both times downward in significance: -0.351 (imposed current, supply only), then +1.6054 (voltage-fed but on the broken lattice), now the value shown. Say plainly that no cutout penalty is frozen as final.

## Slide 27 — Choosing a pad count needs more than voltage drop
The key insight is that the voltage-drop floor of 4 pads is not a design point at all: it implies 5e6 A/cm2, five times the ACCELERATED Cu-Cu electromigration test regime. Current density, not voltage drop, bounds the count from below.

## Slide 28 — Model fidelity should follow the quantity, not the budget
The honest outcome here is a retirement, not a new result. Emphasise that the router was fixed FIRST and proven capable, because otherwise 'R1 equals R0' would be indistinguishable from 'R1 is broken'.

## Slide 29 — "Validated" is not one thing
This is the slide that will earn credit. Convergence is not accuracy. Source reproduction is not validation. If a committee member conflates them, point here.

## Slide 30 — Where the computational effort goes
Be explicit that these timings belong to the frozen geometry and the corrected pair has not been re-benchmarked. Do not let anyone quote these as current.

## Slide 31 — Is it faster than a commercial tool?
Expect to be pushed here. The defensible position: literature speedups from other frameworks are context only and are never transferred, and absent a matched run we report absolute runtime instead of a ratio. Say you would run the matched comparison given solver access.

## Slide 32 — The historical frozen result
Be careful and precise: the counterexample is valid INSIDE the historical model. The corrected physical pair has not been re-run, so do not assert the ordering survives. That honesty is the point.

## Slide 33 — Where the project stands
Frame this as a map, not a confession. The gates exist so that nothing can be quietly declared done. Point out that zero gates are VALIDATED and that this is deliberate honesty, not an absence of work.

## Slide 34 — The path to a final architecture comparison
End the technical portion here. The message: the remaining work is sequenced, not open-ended, and the sequencing is a deliberate methodological choice.
